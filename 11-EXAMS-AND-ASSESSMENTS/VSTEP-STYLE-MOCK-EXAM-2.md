### Passage 2 — Questions 11–20

> **Why board games are back**
>
> In an age dominated by smartphones and streaming services, one might expect board games to have disappeared long ago. Instead, they are enjoying something of a revival. Board game cafés — where customers pay a small fee to play from a library of hundreds of games — have opened in many cities, including Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, and sales of modern board games have grown steadily over the past decade.
>
> Part of the explanation lies in the games themselves. Many people still associate board games with simple childhood classics based mainly on luck. Modern games, by contrast, often involve complex strategy, cooperation and storytelling. Some require players to work together against the game rather than compete against one another, which appeals to people who dislike direct conflict.
>
> Another factor is the desire for face-to-face interaction. Ironically, the more time people spend communicating through screens, the more some of them seem to value activities that bring them physically together. Sitting around a table, laughing, arguing and making decisions as a group provides a kind of social experience that is difficult to replicate online. For young adults in particular, board game cafés offer an alternative to bars and karaoke as a place to meet friends.
>
> Board games have also found a place in education. Teachers use them to develop skills such as planning, negotiation and mathematical reasoning. Because games give immediate feedback and encourage players to try again after failing, they can make learning feel less stressful.
>
> Nevertheless, the trend has its critics. Some argue that modern board games are becoming too expensive, with popular titles costing as much as a video game. Others point out that complicated rules can make newcomers feel excluded. Café owners have responded by employing "game guides", staff whose job is to explain rules and recommend suitable games.
>
> Whether the revival will last remains to be seen. For now, however, it seems that many people are rediscovering a simple pleasure: spending an evening around a table with friends, with their phones — at least for a while — put away.

### Passage 3 — Questions 21–30

> **The truth about recycling plastic**
>
> For many people, putting a plastic bottle in the recycling bin feels like a small but meaningful act of environmental responsibility. The reality, however, is more complicated than most of us realise.
>
> To begin with, only certain types of plastic can be recycled economically. Bottles made from PET — the clear plastic used for most drinks — and some rigid containers are relatively easy to process. Many other items, such as thin plastic bags, food wrappers and containers made from mixed materials, are difficult or expensive to recycle, and often end up in landfill or are burned.
>
> Contamination is another major obstacle. When plastic items are thrown away with food still inside them, or when non-recyclable materials are mixed in, entire batches of recycling may be rejected. In some places, a significant proportion of material collected for recycling is never actually recycled for this reason.
>
> Even when plastic is successfully recycled, it usually cannot be recycled indefinitely. Each time plastic is processed, its quality tends to decline, so recycled plastic is often turned into lower-value products such as clothing fibres or park benches, which may not be recycled again. This process is sometimes described as "downcycling".
>
> None of this means that recycling is pointless. Recycling PET bottles, for example, can save energy and reduce the demand for new plastic. But many environmental experts argue that recycling should be seen as the last option rather than the first. The most effective strategy, they say, is to reduce the amount of plastic we use in the first place and to reuse items wherever possible — an approach summarised by the familiar slogan "reduce, reuse, recycle", in that order.
>
> Governments and companies also have an important role to play. Some countries have introduced deposit schemes, in which customers pay a small extra amount for a drink and get it back when they return the bottle. Such schemes have been associated with much higher collection rates. Others have banned certain single-use items altogether. Ultimately, solving the plastic problem will require changes not only in individual behaviour but also in how products are designed and sold.

### Passage 4 — Questions 31–40

> **When languages disappear**
>
> There are estimated to be around 7,000 languages spoken in the world today, but they are far from equally distributed. A small number of major languages are spoken by the majority of the world's population, while thousands of others have only a few thousand speakers — or far fewer. Linguists warn that a large proportion of these smaller languages may stop being spoken within this century.
>
> Languages rarely disappear suddenly. More commonly, the process takes place over several generations. It often begins when a community comes into contact with a more dominant language that is associated with education, employment and social status. Parents, hoping to give their children better opportunities, may begin speaking the dominant language at home. Their children grow up understanding the original language but speaking it less, and the following generation may not learn it at all. When the last fluent speakers die, the language is said to be extinct.
>
> Why should this matter? Linguists argue that each language represents a unique way of understanding the world. Languages contain knowledge about local plants, animals and environments that may not be recorded anywhere else. They also carry stories, songs and traditions that form part of a community's identity. When a language disappears, much of this knowledge and heritage may be lost with it.
>
> Fortunately, the decline of a language is not always irreversible. The most frequently cited example is Hebrew, which for centuries was used mainly in religious and written contexts before being revived as an everyday spoken language in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Today it has millions of native speakers. Welsh offers another example: after a long period of decline, it has been supported through bilingual education, Welsh-language media and official recognition, and considerable efforts have been made to increase the number of young speakers.
>
> Successful revivals tend to share certain features. Most importantly, they depend on the commitment of the community itself; outside support alone is rarely enough. Teaching the language to young children is also essential, since a language can only survive if it is passed on. Technology is increasingly playing a role as well, with apps, online dictionaries and social media allowing scattered speakers to communicate and learners to practise.
>
> Not every endangered language can be saved, and critics sometimes question whether resources should be spent on languages with very few speakers. Yet supporters argue that the cost of losing a language — which can never be fully recovered — is far greater than the cost of trying to preserve it.
