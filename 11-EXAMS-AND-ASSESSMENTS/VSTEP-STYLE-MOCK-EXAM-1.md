# VSTEP-style Mock Exam 1 — Đề mô phỏng định dạng VSTEP (bậc 3–5)

> ⚠️ **Đề mô phỏng, KHÔNG phải đề thi VSTEP chính thức.** Đề do giáo trình tự biên soạn, mô phỏng **cấu trúc chung** của bài thi VSTEP bậc 3–5 theo các mô tả định dạng được công bố rộng rãi (4 kỹ năng: Nghe ~40 phút/35 câu; Đọc 60 phút/40 câu; Viết 60 phút/2 bài; Nói ~12 phút/3 phần). Nội dung câu hỏi, độ khó và cách chấm **không** được kiểm định bởi cơ quan tổ chức thi, nên **không quy đổi được ra bậc VSTEP** hay điểm chính thức. Trước khi đăng ký thi thật, hãy kiểm tra quy định và định dạng mới nhất từ đơn vị tổ chức thi được cấp phép.

### Passage 1 — Questions 1–10

> **Riverside Community Garden: Volunteers Wanted**
>
> Five years ago, the empty land behind Riverside Primary School was little more than a car park for delivery vans. Today it is home to more than forty vegetable beds, a small fruit orchard and a children's learning area. The transformation is the work of local residents, who began meeting every Sunday morning to clear rubbish and plant the first seeds.
>
> The garden is now managed by a committee of eight volunteers. Each family that joins is given a small bed to look after, in return for a yearly fee of 200,000 dong, which pays for water, tools and seeds. Families who cannot afford the fee may instead contribute four hours of work per month on shared areas such as the orchard.
>
> One of the garden's most popular activities is the Saturday "Grow and Learn" session, during which pupils from the primary school learn how to plant, water and harvest vegetables. Teachers say the sessions have encouraged children to eat more vegetables — "even the ones they used to refuse," one teacher joked.
>
> The garden also donates part of its harvest. Every month, around thirty kilograms of vegetables are delivered to a nearby care home for elderly residents. In return, several residents of the care home visit the garden to share their knowledge of traditional growing methods.
>
> However, the garden faces challenges. The original group of volunteers is getting older, and some have moved away. In addition, last year's heavy rains destroyed nearly half the crops, and the drainage system now needs to be improved. The committee estimates that repairs will cost around 15 million dong.
>
> The committee is now looking for new volunteers, especially young people with skills in social media, fundraising or carpentry. "We don't expect people to be experts," said committee chair Mrs Hoa. "All we ask is enthusiasm and a couple of hours a week." Anyone interested can attend an open morning on the first Sunday of next month, or contact the committee by email.

### Passage 2 — Questions 11–20

> **How coffee came to shape Vietnam**
>
> It is commonly said that coffee was introduced to Vietnam by French missionaries in the mid-nineteenth century. At first, it was grown on a small scale, mainly to supply French residents. It was not until the early twentieth century that larger plantations were established, particularly in the cool, fertile highlands of the Central region.
>
> For many decades, coffee remained a relatively minor crop. The real transformation came after the economic reforms of the late 1980s, when farmers were allowed greater freedom to choose what to grow and to sell their produce on the open market. Encouraged by rising global demand, thousands of families in the Central Highlands began planting coffee. Within little more than a decade, Vietnam had become one of the world's largest coffee exporters — a position it still holds today.
>
> Most of the coffee grown in Vietnam is robusta, a variety that is hardier than arabica and better suited to lower altitudes and hotter conditions. Robusta has a stronger, more bitter taste and contains more caffeine. Much of it is used in instant coffee and blends sold around the world, which means that many people drink Vietnamese coffee without realising it.
>
> Inside the country, coffee has developed a culture of its own. Because fresh milk was historically scarce, people began to sweeten their coffee with condensed milk, creating the drink now famous as *cà phê sữa đá*. In recent years, creative variations such as egg coffee and coconut coffee have attracted tourists, while a new generation of specialty cafés has begun promoting high-quality local arabica.
>
> The rapid expansion of coffee farming has not been without costs, however. In some areas, forests were cleared to make way for plantations, and heavy irrigation has put pressure on groundwater supplies. Moreover, because many farmers depend on a single crop, a sudden fall in world prices can have serious consequences for their incomes. Experts increasingly recommend that farmers diversify — for example by growing pepper or fruit trees alongside coffee — to reduce their risk.

### Passage 3 — Questions 21–30

> **Is remote work here to stay?**
>
> When offices around the world closed in 2020, millions of employees discovered that they could perform much of their work from home. Several years later, the question facing businesses is no longer whether remote work is possible, but whether it is desirable.
>
> Surveys consistently suggest that most employees value the flexibility of working from home at least part of the time. The absence of a daily commute saves time and money, and many workers report a better balance between their professional and personal lives. Parents, in particular, appreciate being able to take their children to school or be at home when they return.
>
> Employers' views are more mixed. Some companies have embraced remote work enthusiastically, reducing office space and recruiting staff from a much wider geographical area. Others remain concerned about productivity and, perhaps more importantly, about company culture. Managers often worry that new employees, who have never met their colleagues in person, may find it harder to learn informally or to feel part of a team.
>
> Research on productivity has produced contradictory findings. Some studies have found that remote workers complete more tasks, possibly because they face fewer interruptions. Others suggest that collaboration and innovation suffer when teams rarely meet. Part of the difficulty is that productivity is hard to measure in jobs that involve creativity or complex problem-solving.
>
> As a result, many organisations have settled on a "hybrid" model, in which employees spend some days in the office and others at home. This compromise is not without its own problems. If everyone chooses to work from home on Mondays and Fridays, offices may be overcrowded midweek and empty at other times. Some companies have responded by introducing "anchor days", on which entire teams are expected to be present.
>
> What seems likely is that there will be no single answer. The right balance will depend on the nature of the work, the needs of individual employees and the culture each organisation wishes to create. What has changed permanently is that employees now expect to be part of the conversation.

### Passage 4 — Questions 31–40

> **The puzzle of the placebo**
>
> In medicine, a placebo is a treatment that has no active ingredient — a sugar pill, for instance, or an injection of salt water. Logically, such treatments should have no effect. Yet for decades, doctors and researchers have observed that patients who receive placebos often report real improvements in their symptoms. This phenomenon, known as the placebo effect, remains one of the most intriguing puzzles in modern science.
>
> The effect appears to be strongest for conditions in which subjective experience plays a major role, such as pain, anxiety and nausea. It is considerably weaker, if present at all, for conditions that can be measured objectively, such as tumour size or blood cholesterol. This suggests that placebos do not cure diseases so much as change the way patients experience them.
>
> Several factors seem to influence the strength of the response. Expectation is clearly important: patients who believe a treatment will work are more likely to feel better. Remarkably, the appearance of the treatment can also matter. Some studies have found that injections tend to produce stronger effects than pills, and that more expensive-looking treatments are perceived as more effective than cheaper ones. The behaviour of the doctor plays a role too; patients who are treated with warmth and attention often report better outcomes.
>
> Perhaps most surprising is the finding that placebos may work even when patients know they are taking one. In so-called "open-label" placebo trials, participants are told openly that their pills contain no medicine. Even so, some trials have reported improvements, particularly in conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome. Researchers are cautious about these results, which come from relatively small studies, but they challenge the assumption that deception is necessary for the effect to occur.
>
> The placebo effect raises important ethical questions. Should doctors ever prescribe a treatment they know to be inactive? Most medical guidelines discourage deliberate deception, as it may damage the trust between doctor and patient. However, some researchers argue that understanding the placebo effect could help doctors improve real treatments — for example, by paying more attention to how they communicate with patients.
>
> There is also a darker side, known as the nocebo effect. When patients expect negative side effects, they are more likely to experience them, even from an inactive substance. This may explain why some people stop taking useful medicines after reading long lists of possible side effects. Clearly, the mind's influence on health works in both directions.
