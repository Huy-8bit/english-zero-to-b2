### Transcript L03-T2 — A radio talk about sleep

> Many parents complain that their teenage children stay up late and can't get out of bed in the morning. They often think this is simply laziness. However, scientists have found that during adolescence, the body clock actually shifts. Teenagers naturally start to feel sleepy later at night — often not until eleven or later — and their bodies want to sleep later in the morning. This means that when school starts at seven, many teenagers are seriously short of sleep, which can affect their concentration, mood and even their health. Some schools in other countries have experimented with starting classes later, and several have reported that students are more alert. Of course, teenagers can also help themselves — for example, by avoiding bright screens just before bed. But the main message is this: teenagers' sleep patterns are different, and schools should take that into account.

### Transcript L08-P3 — The bicycle in cities

> Today I want to talk about the bicycle and its surprising return to city life. In the early twentieth century, bicycles were a common way to get around in many cities. However, in the decades after the Second World War, cars became much more affordable, and many cities were redesigned with wide roads for motor traffic. Cycling became more dangerous and less attractive, and in many places it declined sharply.
>
> More recently, however, cycling has been making a comeback. Concerns about traffic congestion, air pollution and health have encouraged cities to rethink their transport policies. One of the most effective measures has been building separate, protected cycle lanes, so that cyclists don't have to share the road with fast-moving cars. Many cities have also introduced bike-sharing schemes. Their great advantage is that people can use a bicycle without having to buy one or find somewhere to store it at home.
>
> Of course, challenges remain. In cities with hot climates, for example, many people are reluctant to cycle to work because they arrive sweaty and uncomfortable. Electric bikes, which require less effort, may be part of the solution. Overall, the evidence suggests that when cities provide safe and convenient infrastructure, more people will choose to cycle.
