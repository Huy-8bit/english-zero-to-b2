## Unit 1 — A radio interview

> **Presenter:** Good morning. With me today is Dr Mai Tran, who researches the labour market. Dr Mai, should we be worried that robots are going to take our jobs?
>
> **Dr Mai:** Well, I'd say we should be prepared rather than worried. Some jobs will certainly disappear, but history suggests that technology usually changes work more than it destroys it.
>
> **Presenter:** Which jobs are most at risk?
>
> **Dr Mai:** Jobs that consist mainly of routine, repetitive tasks — things like data entry or checking forms. If a task can be described as a clear set of rules, a machine can probably learn to do it.
>
> **Presenter:** Can you give an example of a job that has changed rather than vanished?
>
> **Dr Mai:** Translators are a good example. When translation software improved, many people predicted that translators would become unnecessary. What actually happened is that many translators now spend more time editing machine translations and dealing with cultural nuances that software misses.
>
> **Presenter:** So what skills will people need?
>
> **Dr Mai:** I'd point to three transferable skills: critical thinking, communication, and — above all — the ability to keep learning. Technical knowledge goes out of date quickly; the ability to learn doesn't.
>
> **Presenter:** Some listeners have told us that older workers simply can't learn new technology.
>
> **Dr Mai:** I understand that concern, but it isn't supported by what we see in training programmes. Older workers often learn more slowly at first, but they bring experience and judgement. The real problem is usually lack of access to training, not lack of ability.
>
> **Presenter:** What should governments be doing?
>
> **Dr Mai:** I'd strongly recommend that every adult be given a training budget that they can use throughout their career. It's essential that retraining isn't something people can only afford when they're already unemployed.
>
> **Presenter:** Any final thoughts?
>
> **Dr Mai:** Only that we're a bit late. Had we started this conversation ten years ago, many workers would already be better prepared.

## Unit 2 — A lecture extract

> **Lecturer:** Right, let's move on to what's sometimes called the "sponge city" approach. The basic problem is this: modern cities are covered in concrete and asphalt. When heavy rain falls, the water can't soak into the ground, so it runs off the surface, overwhelming the drains. That's one reason why flash flooding has become so common in many Asian cities.
>
> The idea behind a sponge city is to let the city absorb water like a sponge. This is done through what we call green infrastructure. Let me give you three examples. First, parks designed to flood safely during storms — they're used as playgrounds most of the year and as temporary lakes when it rains heavily. Second, green roofs, which are planted with grass or small plants. And third, permeable pavements, which allow water to pass through into the soil.
>
> There's a second benefit, too. Trees and plants cool the streets, not only by providing shade but also through evaporation — they release water vapour, which lowers the surrounding temperature. So green infrastructure helps with both flooding and the urban heat island effect.
>
> Now, the biggest challenge, particularly in older cities, is space. Historic districts were built very densely, and there's simply very little room for new parks. That's why I'd suggest beginning with pilot schemes on a small scale — a single street or a school car park — and measuring the results carefully before expanding.
>
> **Student:** Isn't all this very expensive?
>
> **Lecturer:** Good question. The initial costs can be high, I admit. But if you compare them with the cost of repairing flood damage year after year, green infrastructure often turns out to be more cost-effective in the long run.

## Unit 3 — A panel discussion

> **Host:** Welcome to *Talking Points*. Tonight: fake news. With me are journalist Long Nguyen and secondary school teacher Ha Pham. Let's start with something simple. Is fake news a serious problem?
>
> **Mr Long:** Absolutely. And I think we'd both agree that it's getting worse, not better.
>
> **Ms Ha:** Yes, I agree with that completely.
>
> **Host:** Mr Long, why does false information spread so quickly?
>
> **Mr Long:** Mainly because it's designed to provoke strong emotions — anger, fear, surprise. People share things that make them feel something, often before they've even finished reading. The truth is usually more complicated and less exciting, so it spreads more slowly.
>
> **Host:** Ms Ha, you teach teenagers. What do you do in class?
>
> **Ms Ha:** One activity I use is giving students two versions of the same story — one real, one invented — and asking them to work out which is which. Then I teach them to ask three questions before sharing anything: Who created this? What evidence is there? And who benefits if I believe it?
>
> **Host:** That sounds very practical. Mr Long, should this be taught in every school?
>
> **Mr Long:** I take Ms Ha's point that education is important. But I'm not convinced it's enough on its own. The platforms themselves have to take more responsibility. You can't expect a fifteen-year-old to fight against algorithms designed by thousands of engineers.
>
> **Ms Ha:** I'd see it slightly differently. Regulation takes years, and platforms change their rules all the time. What students learn in school stays with them for life.
>
> **Host:** So you disagree on where the main responsibility lies. Well, whatever the answer, my advice to our viewers is this: before you share something tonight, stop for ten seconds and ask yourself those three questions.
